# The 28- and 29-vertex terminals at order ten

23 November 2026

The local 28-vertex assertion is false. A balanced ten-coloring on 28 vertices can have **126 target edges**, target independence number three and largest target clique nine. The following construction gives every edge. Here balanced means that every eleven vertices see all ten colors. The 29-vertex bound of 153 remains open in this work. The August terminal family also requires inheritance from a single hypothetical balanced coloring of the full 101 vertices. The example tests the local hypotheses. [Exact replay](verify_order_ten_terminal.py)

## A balanced 28-vertex example

Use nine column vertices $C_0,\dots,C_8$ and nineteen symbols $A_1,\dots,A_8$, $B_0,\dots,B_8$, $u,v$. In a nine-by-nine array the rows are the nine nontarget colors. In row $s$ and column $t$, compute $i=s+t$ and $j=s+2t$ modulo nine. Put the following unordered pair in that cell according to the table below.

| Condition | Pair |
|---|---|
| $i\ne0$ | $A_i,B_j$ |
| $i=0,\ 1\le j\le4$ | $u,B_j$ |
| $i=0,\ 5\le j\le8$ | $v,B_j$ |
| $i=j=0$ | $u,v$ |

Color the triangle consisting of the pair and $C_t$ with hue $s$. The cell pairs are distinct because the map $(s,t)\mapsto(i,j)$ is bijective. No symbol repeats in any row or column: before splitting the zero symbol, both coordinates are permutations along each row and column. Its unique occurrence is the only cell that can use either split symbol. Thus the triangle assignments are consistent. Each column misses exactly one symbol. Give those nine remaining column-to-symbol edges hue zero and every other unassigned edge the target hue.

Every nontarget color has nine vertex-disjoint triangles covering 27 vertices. An independent set in any one such color therefore has at most ten vertices. The graph $H$ of all nontarget edges is the join of the nine independent columns with a triangle-free graph $F$ on the symbols. In $F$, the eight $A$'s are completely joined to the nine $B$'s. The edge $uv$ is present, $u$ sees $B_1,\ldots,B_4$, and $v$ sees $B_5,\ldots,B_8$. There are no further edges. A stable set in $F$ has at most nine points. It uses at most one split symbol, cannot use both an $A$ and a $B$, and a split symbol misses five $B$'s. Consequently $\omega(H)=3$ and $\alpha(H)=9$. Finally
$$
 e_a={28\choose2}-9\cdot19-(8\cdot9+4+4+1)=126.
$$
The checker reconstructs all 378 edges, searches maximum cliques and independent sets in the resulting adjacency matrices, and obtains exactly ten for the independence number of each of the other nine colors. A second check counts all 21,474,180 eleven-sets and finds every color on each.

**No extension of this example to the 29-vertex terminal.** If one extra vertex kept both target restrictions, it would have an $H$-neighbor among the nine columns. Its $H$-neighbors among the symbols must then be independent in $F$, or those two vertices and a symbol edge form an $H$-clique of size four. Yet this independent set must hit each of three independent nine-sets in $F$, namely all nine $B$'s, all eight $A$'s with $u$, and all eight $A$'s with $v$. Hitting a $B$ prevents hitting any $A$, leaving both $u$ and $v$ necessary. They are adjacent. This proves nonextension even if the new star colors are unrestricted subject to the two target parameters.

## The degree-ten branch on 29 vertices

Let $H$ again consist of the nontarget edges. Here $H$ is $K_4$-free and $\alpha(H)\le9$. A triangle-free induced subgraph of $H$ has at most nineteen vertices by the two-row theorem. Thus $d_a(v)\ge9$. The August ledger credits the minimum-degree-nine branch to a private package. The argument below concerns minimum degree ten under a putative $e_a\le152$. These two degrees exhaust the possibilities by averaging. [Two-row theorem, Lemma 9](overfull-core.md#6-a-reduction-to-local-statements-for-any-hue)

Put $q(v)=d_a(v)-10$ and $X=\sum q(v)=2(e_a-145)\le14$. Some $q\ge2$. Indeed, if $\delta(H)\ge17$, saturate $H$ to a maximal $K_4$-free graph. In the nonjoin case Lyle's Theorem 3 gives independence at least $2(2\cdot17-29)=10$. In the join case an independent part has size at most nine and its triangle-free complement has size at least twenty, contradicting the two-row theorem in the original coloring. Also on **28** vertices the same target restrictions imply $\delta(H)\le16$. At degree seventeen Lyle's nonjoin bound gives at least twelve. In the join case the triangle-free part has order $28-i$, where $i\le9$. Its minimum degree is at least $17-i>2(28-i)/5$. The Andrásfai–Erdős–Sós theorem makes it bipartite, yielding an independent ten-set. Hence if $X=2$ on 29 vertices, deleting its unique weight-two vertex would give an impossible 28-vertex graph of minimum $H$-degree at least seventeen. This leaves $147\le e_a\le152$ in this branch. [Lyle, Theorem 3 and Section 5.1](https://www.combinatorics.org/ojs/index.php/eljc/article/download/v21i2p38/pdf/#page=3)

The nine other colors each have at least 28 edges by Turán. Their total surplus over this is $154-e_a\le7$, so at least one is exactly a disjoint union of nine triangles and one pair. Let $k$ be the minimum sum of $q$ on an $H$-triangle. The nine disjoint triangles give $k\le1$. For minimum roots with weights $a_i$, the private rows are target cliques of sizes $9-b_i$. There are $P=2+\sum b_i$ exterior vertices. Root $i$ hits $2+b_i+a_i$ of them and every exterior vertex hits two or three roots. Thus $\sum b_i\le2+k$, and the number hitting all three is $2+k-\sum b_i$. Counting these possibilities gives four rooted profiles at $k=0$ and nine at $k=1$, ordering equal-size rows by root weight. The unweighted tuples are $000,001,002,011$ at zero and $001,002,003,011,012,111$ at one. These are necessary conditions.

There is a further restriction at $k=1$. The zero-weight set induces a triangle-free graph in $H$ and has at least fifteen vertices. For an $H$-edge of zeros, common target neighbors number at most two because its common $H$-neighbors form a target clique of size at most nine, and the two target degrees are ten each. On a shortest odd cycle of length $j\ge5$, each zero sees at most two cycle points in $H$. If there are $z$ zeros, counting edges of the cycle missed at both ends gives $(j-4)z\le2j$, impossible for $z\ge15$. Hence the zeros split into two target cliques and $z\le18$. At least eleven vertices have positive weight, so $X\ge12$ by parity. Thus $k=1$ requires **151 or 152 target edges**. At 151 there are exactly eighteen zeros in two target nine-cliques and the other weights are ten ones and one two, since some weight is at least two.

## A color test at the 72-edge endpoint

At a degree-ten vertex let $S=N_H(x)$. If $H[S]$ is bipartite, its two parts $A,B$ have size nine. When it has 72 edges, each other color is a matching of eight edges there. Each has at least eight by Turán and equality forces a matching with two isolated vertices. Write $a_c\in A,b_c\in B$ for its missing endpoints. Any exterior point missing hue $c$ into $A$ must send $c$ to $b_c$, since the eleven-set consisting of $A$ and those two points must see $c$. The analogous statement holds with the sides exchanged. Thus no exterior point can miss the same hue on both sides. At most two exterior points can miss $c$ on either one side, and if two do, their mutual edge must also be $c$. This gives exact edge requirements shared across all nine hues.

Suppose further that the nine target edges between $A$ and $B$ form a perfect matching. Let $T=N_a(x)$, let $h=e_H(T)$ and let $C=e_a(S,T)$. Each point of $S$ has nine internal target neighbors and needs at least one more in $T$. Counting target edges gives $e_a(W)=81+(55-h)+C$, so $C\le16+h$. At least $36-C\ge20-h$ points of $S$ therefore have exactly one target neighbor in $T$. When $h=9$ or 10, at least ten such points exist. Two, say $s,t$, must be joined in $H$, since a target clique has size at most nine. Their two target neighbors in $T$ together cover every $H[T]$-edge. An uncovered edge would complete an $H$-quartet with $s,t$. A single vertex cannot cover $H[T]$, since the other nine vertices and $x$ would form a target ten-clique. Hence the cover has exactly two points and there is a third target nine-clique, disjoint from $A,B$, consisting of $x$ and the other eight vertices. This excludes every such configuration with three disjoint nontarget edges in $T$.

At $h=9$ each nontarget hue occurs exactly once in $\{x\}\cup T$. For each hue of total size 28 on $W$, equality in Turán supplies a partition into nine triangles and one pair. On $S$ its partition restricts to eight pairs and two singletons. On $\{x\}\cup T$ it restricts to one pair and nine singletons. Both sides meet all ten global blocks. Consequently its lone outside pair must join one of the two singletons of $S$, eight outside singletons must join the eight pairs of $S$, and the remaining singletons on the two sides form its short block. The global surplus bound ensures at least two such hues. Whether these constraints exclude all configurations at 72 edges or the remaining August cases through 77 is open here.
